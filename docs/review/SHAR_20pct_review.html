"Concretely: given a surveillance video, classify it into one of these fourteen classes — thirteen kinds of crime or accident, plus normal activity.

This is hard for three reasons. First, the footage is low resolution, 320 by 240, so the people involved are often only a few dozen pixels tall. Second, the videos are long and untrimmed: the crime may last a few seconds inside several minutes of ordinary footage. Third, several classes look alike — robbery, burglary, stealing and shoplifting differ mainly in context, not in what is visible.

Likith will now show what others have done on this."

Likith4 / 10

"The standard benchmark is UCF-Crime, released at CVPR 2018: 1,900 real surveillance videos. Its authors defined two tasks.

The first is detection — when does something abnormal happen. Almost all later work went here, and it now reports frame-level scores above 85%.

The second is recognition — which crime is it, over fourteen classes. Here the dataset authors reported 23 and 28 percent accuracy, and very few papers followed. The best figure we found, 45 percent, needed frame-level labels added by hand and used a single split."

Research gap

Three gaps we address

1

Recognition is neglected

Nearly all work on UCF-Crime detects when; very little names which crime.

2

Many reported numbers are inflated

Papers quoting 80–90% split clips or frames at random, so the same video sits in training and test.

3

Foundation models are untested here

DINOv2, SigLIP and V-JEPA 2 have not been evaluated on this task under the official protocol.

Likith5 / 10

"So we see three gaps.

One: recognition is far behind detection and has hardly been studied.

Two: many papers quote 80 to 90 percent on 'UCF-Crime', but they split clips or frames at random, so the same video appears in both training and testing. Those numbers do not measure generalisation.

Three: the large pretrained 'foundation' models released in the last two years — DINOv2, SigLIP, V-JEPA 2 — have not been tested on this task under the official protocol.

Vishnu will explain what we plan to do about it."

Vishnu6 / 10

"Our project has three objectives.

One, recognition: classify each video into the 14 classes on the official four-fold split, and report macro-F1 as well as accuracy, so that rare classes count as much as common ones.

Two, robustness to poor image quality: real CCTV is noisy and dark. We will add ten kinds of noise and low light, try ten restoration methods — classical filters, an autoencoder we train ourselves, and pretrained networks — and measure whether cleaning the image actually helps recognition, not only whether it looks better.

Three, region of interest: test whether cropping to the people found by a standard detector helps, compared with using the full frame.

All three use one evaluation rule: no video is ever in both training and testing."

Vishnu7 / 10

"We use the official UCF-Crime release: 1,900 untrimmed videos, about 128 hours, all at 320 by 240.

For recognition we follow the official protocol exactly. It uses 700 videos, 50 per class, in four folds. Each fold trains on 532 videos and tests on 168, and the four test sets do not overlap. We report the average over the four folds.

One honest point: each video has a single label for the whole video. We do not have frame-by-frame labels, so the model must learn which part of the video matters on its own."

Proposed method

Frozen pretrained models do the seeing; only a small classifier is trained

Vishnu8 / 10

"Our plan is deliberately simple. We do not train a large video network from scratch — with 38 training videos per class it would overfit.

Instead, we take public foundation models that were trained once on very large image and video collections, and use them frozen, without changing them. Each one turns a frame or a short clip into a feature vector. We average those vectors over the video, and train a small, fast classifier on top.

To choose between design options without touching the test set, we will use cross-validation inside the training videos only. The test folds are read only at the end.

Abijith will close with the plan."

"To sum up: we are tackling the neglected half of UCF-Crime — naming the crime — with modern pretrained models and an evaluation strict enough that the numbers can be trusted. Thank you; we are happy to take questions."

Why not fine-tune a deep network end to end? With only 38 training videos per class, a large network memorises the training videos. Frozen pretrained features plus a small classifier is the standard answer for small datasets, and we will test fine-tuning against it as a comparison.

Why UCF-Crime and not a bigger dataset? It is the standard real-world benchmark with an official recognition protocol, so our numbers can be compared with published work. We may add a second dataset later as a transfer check.

Why macro-F1 and not only accuracy? Accuracy can look good by getting the easy classes right. Macro-F1 averages over all fourteen classes, so a class like Shooting counts as much as Normal.

Papers report 90% on UCF-Crime. Why is your target lower? Those papers split clips or frames at random, so the same video is in training and test. On the official split, where test videos are never seen in training, published results are between 23% and 45%.

The labels are for the whole video. Isn't that a problem? Yes, and we say so openly: the crime may be a few seconds of a long video. The official temporal annotations exist only for part of the test videos, so we report the video-level score and name it as such.

Why denoising, if the videos are not noisy? Real CCTV often is, especially at night. The question is whether a cleaner-looking image is also easier to recognise — image-quality scores like PSNR and recognition accuracy need not agree, and we will measure both.

What is new in your work? Testing the latest foundation models on this task under the official protocol, a stricter way to choose models without using the test set, and measuring restoration by its effect on recognition rather than on image quality alone.
